The Expert Witness Invoice: What Litigation's Most Expensive Line Item Really Runs

The invoice arrived on a Tuesday: $38,000 for one expert, on a case with $200,000 at stake. The partner stared at it, then at the discovery budget, then at the client. The expert had billed more than the depositions, the document review, and the filing fees combined — and the trial had not started.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 20, 2026 · 7 min read

The invoice arrived on a Tuesday: $48,000 for one expert, on a case with $200,000 at stake. The partner stared at it, then at the discovery budget, then at the client. The expert had billed more than the depositions, the document review, and the filing fees combined — and the trial had not started. The expert witness is the most expensive line item in American litigation, and it is the one most often budgeted last, after the money is already committed.

The benchmark numbers come from SEAK, Inc.'s Survey of Expert Witness Fees, the largest recurring survey of testifying experts, with 1,633 respondents across more than 250 areas of expertise in its 2024 edition. The medians: $450 an hour for file review and case preparation, $475 an hour for deposition testimony, and $500 an hour for trial testimony. The averages run higher — $466, $571, and $633 — because a long tail of very high billers pulls the mean up. The reported highs reach $2,500 an hour for review and deposition, and $3,500 an hour for trial. The median retainer is $3,000, collected before work begins. The median total billing for one expert on one case, start to finish, is $7,000 — a figure that conceals as much as it reveals, because the distribution is so wide.

The specialty spread is wider than the spread between firms. SEAK's data shows plastic surgery experts at $700 an hour for review, $825 for deposition, and $825 for trial. Urology runs $567 for review, $750 for deposition, and $983 for trial. General surgery bills $450 for review, $775 for deposition, and $825 for trial. At the other end, engineering experts bill a flat $333 across all three activities, economics $323 for review, $412 for deposition, and $425 for trial, and transportation $283, $333, and $350. A neurosurgeon and a traffic engineer are both expert witnesses. They are not the same purchase.

The Expert Institute, which places experts for law firms, reports lower placement averages — $356 an hour for review, $448 for deposition, $478 for trial — a reminder that SEAK's figures are self-reported asking rates, not collected billings. ExpertPages' 2026 survey of more than 400 experts puts the average across all fields at $465 an hour. The three sources disagree by tens of dollars, not by multiples, and they agree on the shape: review is cheapest, deposition costs more, trial costs most, and medicine costs more than everything else.

How the invoice is built

The hourly rate is the smallest part of the invoice. The structure around it is where the money accumulates. Most experts charge a retainer — a median of $3,000 — applied against the first hours, and many require replenishment before the deposition. Trial testimony commonly carries a half-day or full-day minimum, typically four hours, so a two-hour court appearance bills as four. Travel is billed at the hourly rate or at half rate, plus expenses, and a trial that requires the expert to wait in the hallway bills the waiting time too. A report, which can take twenty to forty hours to write, bills at the review rate. A rebuttal report bills again. The deposition preparation — reviewing the opposing expert's report, preparing exhibits, a prep session with counsel — bills at the review rate before the deposition rate even starts.

Put the structure together for a medical-malpractice case that reaches trial and the total is the number that shocks clients. Published practice guides put the expert cost for a malpractice case that reaches trial at $15,000 to $30,000, commonly, for a single expert — and most malpractice cases need two or three: the liability expert, the causation expert, and the damages expert. Engineering and construction cases run higher still, $30,000 to $40,000 or more in expert fees alone, because the experts must inspect, test, and model rather than merely review records. A life care plan, the document that projects a catastrophically injured plaintiff's future medical costs, runs $3,000 to $8,000 as a flat fee, before the planner testifies about it.

The invoice also has a failure mode the budget rarely includes: the expert who is excluded. Under Federal Rule of Evidence 702, as amended in 2023, the judge is the gatekeeper, and a Daubert challenge that excludes an expert writes off every dollar spent on that expert — the retainer, the review, the report, the deposition — and leaves the case without the testimony it was built on. Economics and financial damages experts face the highest Daubert-challenge exposure of any specialty. The budget for an expert is not only the fee. It is the fee plus the risk that the fee buys nothing.

The invoice as a share of the case

For a commercial case, the expert can exceed the entire discovery budget. Discovery — document review, depositions, e-discovery — typically runs 10 to 15 percent of total litigation cost. A single medical expert at $500 an hour for trial, with a four-hour minimum, a deposition day, a report, and thirty hours of review, bills roughly $25,000. On a case with $200,000 at stake, that is an eighth of the amount in controversy for one witness. On a case with $50,000 at stake, it is half. The economics explain why small cases settle: the expert costs more than the dispute is worth.

The comparison that matters is not the expert against the discovery budget but the expert against the alternative. A case without an expert is, in most technical disputes, a case without a claim — medical malpractice, product liability, construction defect, and patent cases all require expert testimony as a matter of law. The expert is not a luxury. It is the price of admission, and the price is set by a market with no posted rates, no negotiation norm, and no refund.

The counterargument: the expert is the case

The defense of the invoice is that the expert is not overhead. The expert is the evidence. A jury does not decide a medical-malpractice case on the medical records; it decides it on the expert's explanation of the records. A construction defect case turns on the engineer's model, not on the photographs. The $500 an hour buys twenty years of practice, a reputation the expert stakes on the testimony, and the cross-examination that follows — the expert is paid for the hours in the courtroom and for the career that makes those hours credible. The firms that complain about the invoice are often the firms that hired the cheapest expert and lost the Daubert hearing.

There is a second defense, quieter and more practical: the expert's fee is negotiable in a way the court's schedule is not. Retainers can be capped. Review hours can be limited in the engagement letter. The trial minimum can be negotiated from a full day to a half day. The experts who charge the most are often the ones most willing to agree to a budget, because they have the most to lose from a client who stops paying. The invoice that shocks is usually the invoice that was never discussed before the work began.

What to budget before hiring

Five numbers, agreed in writing before the retainer is paid, will prevent the Tuesday-morning surprise. The review rate, the deposition rate, and the trial rate — separately, because they differ. The retainer and the replenishment trigger. The trial minimum, in hours, and whether waiting time bills. The travel rate and expenses. And the cap, if any, on total billing without further authorization. SEAK's medians — $450, $475, $500 — are the planning numbers. The specialty matters more than the median: a plastic surgeon at $825 an hour for trial will bill twice the median before the deposition starts. The case that budgets the expert last is the case that discovers the expert first, on the invoice, after the money is gone.
