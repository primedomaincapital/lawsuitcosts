In late 2024, the general counsel of a Fortune 100 manufacturing company did something unusual. She put her company’s entire litigation portfolio — 43 active matters, spread across 11 law firms — up for competitive bid. Not the routine panel review that happens every few years. A real auction, with a spreadsheet, deadlines, and a clear message: the firm that couldn’t explain its pricing in plain English wouldn’t be invited back.

Three of the 11 firms declined to participate. They said their rates were their rates. Within eighteen months, two of those three had been replaced on the panel entirely.

This is the new reality for the American legal industry, a roughly $400 billion market that for decades operated on a simple principle: charge by the hour, raise rates every January, and trust that clients would pay. That trust is breaking. Corporate legal departments, armed with better data and tighter budgets, are dismantling the billable hour’s century-long reign — and the firms that can’t adapt are losing the work.

The numbers behind the revolt

The Thomson Reuters Institute’s annual reports have tracked the same pattern for years: law firm billing rates rise 3 to 5 percent annually, consistently outpacing inflation, while corporate legal budgets grow more slowly or flatline. The 2024 edition found that the largest firms — the Am Law 100 — pushed through some of the steepest increases on record, with average standard rates climbing past $1,100 per hour for partners at top firms. First-year associates at elite New York firms now bill at rates that would have been partner-level a decade ago: $750 to $900 per hour, according to industry surveys.

Corporate clients noticed. The Association of Corporate Counsel’s annual benchmarking consistently shows in-house leaders ranking “controlling outside counsel costs” as their top priority. More tellingly, a growing share report they’re simply sending less work outside. The 2024 ACC survey found that the proportion of legal work handled in-house has crept upward for five straight years, now accounting for roughly half of total legal spend at large companies.

The math is brutal for law firms. When a client moves a mid-level litigation matter in-house, the firm doesn’t just lose that matter’s revenue. It loses the training ground where junior associates learned the craft — the same juniors whose billing rates the client was already questioning.

The alternative fee dam breaks

For years, “alternative fee arrangements” were the industry’s favorite buzzword and least-used practice. Law firms talked about fixed fees and success fees in pitches, then billed by the hour anyway. Clients asked for budget certainty, got vague estimates, and paid the overruns.

That’s changing — not because firms had a philosophical awakening, but because procurement departments got involved. When sourcing professionals started applying the same rigor to legal services that they apply to IT contracts and office supplies, the billable hour’s opacity became indefensible. You can’t benchmark an hour. You can benchmark an outcome.

Burford Capital, the world’s largest litigation funder with a portfolio exceeding $7 billion in committed capital, has been both a beneficiary and an accelerant of this shift. By pricing litigation as an investment — with expected returns, timelines, and risk adjustments — funders forced everyone in the ecosystem to think about cases the way businesses think about everything else: what does it cost, what does it return, and is the ratio acceptable?

The result is a Cambrian explosion of pricing models. Fixed fees for defined-scope matters. Phased budgets with kill-or-continue gates. Success fees tied to outcomes. Portfolio arrangements where firms handle dozens of matters for a blended rate. The billable hour isn’t dead — it still dominates complex, unpredictable litigation — but its monopoly is over.

The associate salary trap

Law firms face a structural problem that makes pricing reform genuinely painful. The Cravath system — lockstep compensation, up-or-out promotion, ever-rising associate salaries — requires ever-rising billing rates to sustain. When Cravath, Swaine & Moore set the market at $225,000 for first-year associates in 2021, then $250,000 shortly after, every peer firm had to match or lose talent. Those salaries get passed to clients as higher rates.

But clients are increasingly refusing to pay for the training of someone else’s junior lawyers. The “no first-year” policies that started as whispers a decade ago are now standard RFP language. Microsoft, one of the most sophisticated legal buyers in the world, has long pushed firms on staffing efficiency. Others have followed. The result is a squeezed middle: firms can’t cut associate pay without losing talent, can’t raise rates without losing clients, and can’t staff matters leanly without changing a business model built on leverage.

Some firms are responding by creating entirely new staffing categories — “career associates,” “staff attorneys,” contract lawyers — billed at lower rates than the partnership track. It’s an admission that the old model was overcharging for routine work. Clients knew it all along.

Technology sharpens the knife

Generative AI arrived in legal services at exactly the wrong moment for the billable hour’s defenders. When a task that took a junior associate six hours can be done in forty-five minutes with AI assistance, the question isn’t whether the work product is good enough. It’s what the client should pay for.

Thomson Reuters and LexisNexis have both embedded AI deeply into their research platforms, and law firms are racing to deploy tools from Harvey, CoCounsel, and a dozen startups. The efficiency gains are real. But they create an awkward conversation: if the firm is 40% more efficient, should the bill be 40% lower? Firms argue they’re selling judgment, not hours. Clients argue they were always buying hours, whatever the marketing said.

The honest answer is that AI is accelerating a transition that was already underway. The firms thriving in 2026 aren’t the ones billing the most hours. They’re the ones who figured out how to price judgment, speed, and outcomes — and stopped pretending an hour is a unit of value.

The counterargument: you get what you pay for

The legal industry’s defenders make a serious point. In high-stakes litigation — bet-the-company cases, complex regulatory matters, multi-district mass torts — experience matters enormously, and experience is expensive. The partner who has tried 50 cases to verdict is not interchangeable with the one who has tried five. Hourly billing, for all its flaws, at least attempts to price that differential.

There’s also a selection effect. The matters most amenable to fixed fees are the routine ones. The matters that still bill hourly are the unpredictable, complex, high-risk ones — exactly where clients least want budget surprises and most need experienced counsel. Pushing too hard on price can drive the best lawyers away from the hardest problems.

Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz — perennially the most profitable firm in America — has never competed on price and never will. Its clients pay premium rates because the alternative is worse. For truly elite counsel in truly critical matters, the pricing conversation is short.

What this means for litigation economics

The legal industry is bifurcating. At the top, a small group of elite firms commands premium rates for premium work, largely insulated from pricing pressure. In the middle, firms face an existential squeeze between rising costs and resistant clients. At the bottom, technology and alternative providers are commoditizing routine work at unprecedented speed.

For corporate legal departments, this is mostly good news. More pricing transparency, more competition, more options. For law firms, it’s the most significant business-model disruption in a century. The billable hour survived the 2008 financial crisis, when clients made the same noises about cost control and then went back to paying. It may not survive the combination of procurement discipline, AI efficiency, and a generation of general counsel who grew up questioning why legal services should be the only thing they buy without knowing the price in advance.

The Fortune 100 GC who auctioned her litigation portfolio got what she wanted: clearer pricing, better alignment, lower total spend. The firms that participated got something too — a preview of how every sophisticated client will buy legal services within five years. The ones who declined to bid learned a different lesson. In this market, “our rates are our rates” is not a pricing strategy. It’s a resignation letter.
