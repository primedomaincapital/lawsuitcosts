The 33 Percent Question: What Contingency Fees Really Cost

A client wins a $300,000 settlement and walks away with less than half of it. Nobody stole anything. The fee agreement said 40 percent, the case costs came off the top, the medical liens came off what was left, and the arithmetic was all in writing. The client signed it two years earlier, on the worst day of his life, without reading past the first page.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 9, 2026 · 5 min read

The contingency fee is the most important price in American litigation, and the least understood by the people who pay it. The standard is 33⅓ percent of the gross recovery if the case settles before a lawsuit is filed, rising to 40 percent once litigation begins, and up to 45 percent for cases that go through trial or appeal. The American Bar Association describes the typical range as one-third to 40 percent, and the New York City Bar Legal Referral Service identifies 33 percent as the ordinary percentage for a personal injury case. No national rule sets the number. It is set by the agreement, and it must be reasonable under ABA Model Rule 1.5, which also requires the agreement in writing, signed by the client, stating the percentages and whether costs come out before or after the fee.

That last clause is where the money hides. A fee taken from the gross recovery is larger than a fee taken from the net, and on a $100,000 settlement the difference between gross and net fee calculation can swing $5,000 or more. Case costs — filing fees, experts, depositions, medical records — are usually advanced by the lawyer and repaid from the recovery, and whether they are deducted before or after the percentage is applied changes the client's share by thousands. Medical liens are often negotiable, and a lawyer who negotiates them down is worth more than a lawyer who charges a point less.

Where 33 percent is illegal

Several states override the market rate by statute, mostly in medical malpractice. California's MICRA, amended in 2022 by AB 35, caps the fee at 25 percent if the claim resolves before a lawsuit is filed and 33 percent after filing. New York's Judiciary Law §474-a imposes a sliding scale on medical malpractice recoveries: 30 percent of the first $250,000, 25 percent of the next $250,000, 20 percent of the next $500,000, 15 percent of the next $250,000, and 10 percent of anything over $1,250,000. Illinois caps medical malpractice fees at one-third. Connecticut applies a sliding scale from 33⅓ percent of the first $300,000 down to 10 percent above $1.2 million. Twenty-three of the 51 U.S. jurisdictions limit what a plaintiff's lawyer may charge on contingency in a medical malpractice case.

Federal claims have their own ceilings. The Federal Tort Claims Act caps fees at 20 percent of an administrative settlement and 25 percent of a judgment or a settlement after suit is filed. Camp Lejeune Justice Act claims carry the same 20 and 25 percent limits. Florida's bar rules set a sliding scale in personal injury — 33⅓ percent of a recovery up to $1 million before the defendant answers, 40 percent after the answer, and an extra 5 percent if an appeal is needed — and fees above those levels are presumed excessive.

The study that complicates the story

A 2026 Insurance Research Council study of 7.4 million claims from 2017 to 2022 found that represented bodily injury claimants net just $1.40 per dollar of medical bills paid after fees and costs, versus $1.80 for unrepresented claimants. The finding is real, and it is also easy to misread. Represented claimants tend to have larger, more complex injuries and larger medical bills, and the study measures net per dollar of medical expense, not total recovery. A claimant who nets $1.40 per dollar on a $200,000 case is better off than one who nets $1.80 per dollar on a $20,000 case. The fee buys leverage, and leverage shows up in the size of the recovery, not in the ratio.

That is the honest tension at the center of the contingency fee. It is the only financing mechanism that lets a person with no money sue a defendant with unlimited money, and it is also a price that most clients agree to under stress, without shopping, in a market where the price is rarely advertised and almost never negotiated. Both facts are true at once.

The counterargument: the fee is the access

The defense of the contingency fee is not sentimental. It is arithmetic. A lawyer who takes a case on contingency advances tens of thousands of dollars in costs, works for years without pay, and earns nothing if the case loses. The 40 percent reflects that risk, and the market for that risk is what keeps the courthouse open to people who cannot pay an hourly rate. Caps, for all their consumer appeal, can price the hardest cases out of the market: a complex medical malpractice case with a capped fee and a six-figure expert bill is a case many firms will decline.

Florida's experience makes the point. Its medical liability fee limit sits in the state constitution, but clients routinely waive it on a prescribed, notarized form under the Bar rule, and most medical malpractice firms operate under the general 33⅓ and 40 percent schedule after obtaining the waiver. The cap exists. The market routes around it. That tells you something about where the real price is set.

What to ask before signing

First, is the percentage applied to the gross or the net recovery, and are case costs deducted before or after the fee? Second, what is the fee at each stage — pre-suit, post-filing, trial, appeal — and does it step up automatically? Third, who pays the costs if the case loses, and is that in writing? Fourth, does the state cap the fee for this kind of case, and is the agreement consistent with the cap? Fifth, will the lawyer negotiate the medical liens, and is that part of the service or an extra charge?

The contingency fee is not a scam and it is not a bargain. It is a price for risk, set in a market where the buyer rarely shops and the seller rarely advertises. The clients who do best are the ones who treat the fee agreement like the financial document it is, and read it before the worst day of their life, not after.
