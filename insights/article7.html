How Long a Commercial Lawsuit Takes, in Months and in Dollars

The median federal civil case that reaches trial takes 33.7 months to get there — and fewer than one case in two hundred ever sees a courtroom. The bill arrives monthly, long before any verdict. Here is what each stage costs.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 6 min read

A general counsel signed a litigation budget in January. The case was a commercial contract dispute, $800,000 at stake, straightforward facts, a defendant with money. The budget said eighteen months and $150,000. Thirty-four months later the case settled on the courthouse steps, and the bill was $310,000. The budget was not dishonest. It was built on the wrong clock — the clock of the case the general counsel hoped for, rather than the clock of the case the docket actually runs.

The federal docket's clock is published, and it is slower than most budgets assume. The Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts' Federal Court Management Statistics, for the twelve months ending June 30, 2026, put the median time from filing to trial for civil cases at 33.7 months. The median time from filing to disposition — any ending, including settlement, dismissal, or judgment — was 15.6 months for the 355,243 cases terminated in the year ending September 30, 2025. Cases that ended with no court action resolved in a median of 5.1 months. Cases that ended after court action before pretrial took 18.8 months. Cases that reached trial took 30.7 months to disposition in that series. The 33.7-month figure and the 30.7-month figure come from different tables covering different periods; both say the same thing, which is that a case that goes the distance takes roughly three years.

Almost none do. Of the 407,379 civil cases terminated in the year ending September 30, 2025, 0.4 percent reached trial. In the year ending March 2025, the share was 0.3 percent. The system runs on settlement, dismissal, and summary judgment — not on trials. A budget built for trial is a budget built for an event with a 1-in-250 probability, and the money is spent long before the event arrives.

The stages, and what each one costs

The case moves in stages, and each stage has its own clock and its own bill. Filing and service cost little: the federal filing fee is $405, and state filing fees generally run $200 to $450. The first real money is the complaint and the answer, fifty to a hundred hours of partner and associate time. At 2026 market rates — $150 to $275 an hour for junior associates, $275 to $450 for mid-levels, $450 to $700 for senior attorneys, $700 to $1,500 or more for partners — the pleadings alone can run $15,000 to $40,000 on a contract dispute under $100,000.

Discovery is where the money goes. The discovery phase typically takes six to eighteen months, and it is the most expensive phase of litigation. Depositions require a court reporter at $150 to $400 per session plus $4.50 to $7.50 per page of transcript; a single deposition transcript can run 100 to 300 pages. Document production and e-discovery run $10,000 to $500,000 or more depending on volume. Expert witnesses — the subject of the companion piece in this series — add $15,000 to $50,000 each. On a moderate civil case with 500 attorney hours, two experts, and five depositions, a litigation cost estimator puts attorney fees at about $145,000 and total costs at about $206,000. Non-attorney costs — experts, depositions, discovery, filing fees — run 25 to 40 percent of the total.

Motions practice is the next toll. A summary judgment motion, with briefing on both sides and a hearing, can consume a hundred hours. A case that survives summary judgment is a case that has already cost most of its budget, which is why so many settle at that point: the parties have spent the money and learned the facts, and the remaining question is whether the last 20 percent of the budget is worth the chance of a better number at trial.

Trial is the most expensive stage per day and the shortest stage in the case. Trial preparation — exhibits, witness preparation, jury consultants, daily transcripts — adds a premium of 100 to 200 percent over the cost of the same hours in discovery. A commercial case over $250,000 that runs through discovery and trial is estimated at $71,000 to $227,000 in total per side in Manhattan market data, with attorney fees of $50,000 to $150,000 and discovery and experts of $20,000 to $75,000. A patent case can run $200,000 to $3 million or more. The monthly burn rate — total cost divided by duration — is the number the general counsel should have signed in January: for a $206,000 case over 24 months, about $8,600 a month, every month, whether or not anything visible happens.

The faster alternative, and its price

Arbitration is the comparison most often offered, and the numbers support it on speed. The American Arbitration Association reports that large business cases over $1 million reached an award in a median of 18.7 months — against about 34 months to trial in the federal courts. The gap is real, and it is worth roughly fifteen months of burn rate, or about $130,000 on the moderate case above. Arbitration is not free — the arbitrator's fees, the filing fees, and the hearing costs are real — but the clock is the saving, and the clock is where the money goes.

State court is the other comparison, and it is slower in the places that matter. The Bureau of Justice Statistics, in the most recent national tort data, found a median of about 14 months from filing to disposition for tort cases in large state courts, with trial cases taking nearly two years — and a median of 20.1 months from filing to verdict in car accident trials. Those figures are from 2005 trial data, and they describe proportions rather than current waiting times. The federal numbers are current. The state numbers are a reminder that the docket, not the lawyer, sets the pace.

The counterargument: time is also leverage

There is a case for the slow clock, and defendants make it constantly. Time favors the party with deeper pockets and more patience. A plaintiff who needs the money settles cheaper in month eighteen than in month six. A defendant who can wait out the plaintiff's burn rate buys the settlement at a discount. The delay is not only a cost; it is a negotiating instrument, and the side that understands that is the side that prices the settlement correctly. The plaintiff's lawyer who promises a fast resolution is often the lawyer who has not read the docket.

The honest version is that the timeline is the budget. A case that takes 33.7 months to trial does not cost 33.7 months of effort; it costs 33.7 months of carrying cost — the retainer replenished, the experts retained, the documents preserved, the witnesses kept warm — plus the bursts of intense spending at each stage. The general counsel who budgets by stage and by month, rather than by outcome, is the one whose January budget survives to December.

What the clock means for the decision

Three numbers should sit on top of every litigation budget. The median time to disposition for the case type, the median time to trial, and the monthly burn rate. The federal medians are 15.6 months to disposition and 33.7 months to trial. The burn rate for a moderate commercial case is roughly $8,000 to $9,000 a month. Multiply the burn rate by the months to the next decision point — not to trial, but to the next settlement window — and that is the price of waiting. The settlement that looks expensive in month six looks cheap in month thirty, after the discovery bill, the expert bill, and the summary judgment bill have all arrived. The case settles on the courthouse steps because the courthouse steps are where the arithmetic finally becomes undeniable.
