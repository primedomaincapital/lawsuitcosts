The Settlement Discount: What Plaintiffs Give Up to Avoid Trial, in Numbers

Ninety-five percent of personal injury cases settle before trial, and the ones that do not are not a random sample. The median jury award is $100,000. The plaintiffs who roll the dice win barely half the time — and the discount for certainty is the price of the system working.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 20, 2026 · 7 min read

An Alabama mother rejected a $400,000 pre-trial settlement offer in 2024 after an eighteen-wheeler rear-ended her vehicle. The jury awarded $2,812,000 — seven times the insurer's offer. The story travels well, and it is true. It is also the exception that proves the rule, because the rule is that the plaintiff who rejects the settlement usually gets less, not more — and the data on what plaintiffs give up to avoid trial is more precise than the anecdote suggests.

About 95 percent of personal injury cases settle before trial. Only 4 to 5 percent reach a verdict. The split is not an accident. It is the market's verdict on the trial itself: settlement is faster, cheaper, and certain, and trial is slower, more expensive, and uncertain. The question is not whether settlement is usually right. It is how large the discount is, and when the discount is worth refusing.

The baseline numbers come from Thomson Reuters Jury Verdict Research, published by the Insurance Information Institute. Across personal injury trials from 2014 to 2020, the median jury award was $100,000. The mean jury award for the same period was $1,479,368 — a fourteenfold gap driven by a small number of catastrophic-injury verdicts. The middle 50 percent of all awards ran from $20,000 to $505,000. The median is the number that describes the typical case. The mean is the number that describes the headlines. A plaintiff who compares a settlement offer to the mean is comparing it to a number that almost no case achieves.

Product liability sits at the expensive end of the distribution. Jury Verdict Research has reported a median compensatory award of $1,531,961 in product liability trials specifically, with transportation-product cases at $3,000,000 — figures from an earlier study, with current product-specific medians not publicly available. The mass-tort programs anchor the top of the market: 3M's $6.01 billion earplug settlement closed its federal multidistrict litigation in April 2026, Bayer has paid about $11 billion resolving roughly 100,000 Roundup claims, and Philips agreed to $1.1 billion for CPAP claims in April 2024. Individual payouts inside those programs run far smaller than the headlines — $5,000 to $300,000 per claimant in the 3M program, up to $700,000 for the most severe cases. The headline is the program. The check is the claimant.

The trial penalty, measured

The gap between settlement and verdict on the same facts is usually expressed as a multiple: verdicts run two to five times higher than settlements on the same fact pattern, according to compiled verdict and settlement database analysis. That multiple is real, and it is also misleading, because it compares the verdicts that happened with the settlements that happened — and the cases that go to trial are not the cases that settle. They are the cases where the parties disagreed most about value, where the injuries were most severe, or where one side misjudged the jury. The multiple measures selection as much as it measures the trial premium.

The cleaner measure is the win rate. Plaintiffs win only about 50 to 60 percent of trials overall, varying widely by case type. In product liability, RAND Institute for Civil Justice data puts the plaintiff win rate at 44 percent. In medical malpractice, the defense wins roughly nine in ten trials, according to American Medical Association data. Jury Verdict Research found plaintiffs winning 47 percent of Alabama jury trials, with an average personal injury verdict of $309,062. The expected value of a trial is the verdict multiplied by the probability of winning, minus the cost of getting there — and the cost of getting there, in expert fees, depositions, and trial preparation, is the part the multiple ignores.

Run the arithmetic on the median case. A $100,000 median verdict, a 50 percent win rate, and $25,000 in trial costs yields an expected value of $25,000 — before the contingency fee. A settlement offer of $60,000, net of a 33 percent fee, puts $40,000 in the client's pocket. The trial that "pays more" pays more only if the plaintiff wins, and the plaintiff wins about half the time. The settlement discount is the price of converting a coin flip into cash.

What the discount buys

Time is the first thing the discount buys. Cases that settle before trial resolve about 40 percent faster than those that proceed to trial, according to 2024 data. A slip-and-fall case that settles can close in months; the same case at trial in California can take two to four years to reach a jury, and three to five years before the money arrives after appeals. The contingency fee is the second thing: 33.3 percent before suit, rising to 40 percent in litigation, on a $150,000 settlement is $49,950 to $60,000 before case costs. The fee is charged on the recovery, and a larger recovery at trial carries a larger fee. The client's net, not the gross verdict, is the number that matters, and the fee schedule narrows the gap between settling and winning.

Certainty is the third. A settlement is a number the plaintiff knows before signing. A trial is a number the plaintiff learns after two years of waiting, and the number can be zero. The Alabama mother who won seven times the offer is the story that gets told. The plaintiff who rejected the offer and lost is the story that does not get told, because there is no press release for a defense verdict and no fee for the lawyer. The published verdicts are a selected sample of the trials that produced large numbers. The settlements are the silent majority.

The counterargument: when the discount is too steep

There are cases where the settlement discount is not worth paying, and the data says so plainly. When the defendant's offer is below the cost of the case — when the medical bills alone exceed the settlement — the plaintiff loses money by settling, and the trial, for all its risk, is the only path to a number that covers the loss. When the case turns on a principle the defendant will repeat — a defective product still on the market, a practice still in use — the trial's public verdict has a value the confidential settlement does not. And when the defendant's offer reflects not the value of the case but the plaintiff's exhaustion — the two years of waiting, the depositions, the medical examinations — the discount is not a price. It is a tactic, and the correct response is to wait.

The Alabama case is the illustration. The $400,000 offer was made before trial on a case with a back injury requiring surgery, chronic pain, reduced earnings, and a defense that tried to shift liability to a third vehicle and failed. The jury awarded $2,812,000. The offer was not a fair discount for certainty. It was a lowball, and the plaintiff's lawyer knew it, because the liability was clear and the damages were documented. The difference between that case and the median case is the difference between a strong case and a typical one — and the settlement decision turns on which kind of case the plaintiff has.

How to price the decision

The framework is the expected value, computed honestly. Take the realistic verdict range for the case type and venue — the median, not the mean. Multiply by the win rate for the case type. Subtract the remaining trial costs, including the expert fees and the trial preparation premium. Subtract the fee at the trial rate, not the settlement rate. Compare the result with the settlement offer net of the settlement fee. If the trial's expected net exceeds the settlement's net by more than the value of two more years of waiting, go to trial. If it does not, settle. The calculation is not complicated. The discipline to run it, with the median rather than the headline, is the hard part. The settlement discount is real, and it is usually worth paying. The cases where it is not are the cases where the offer is not a discount at all, but a price for the plaintiff's patience — and patience, unlike a verdict, is free.
